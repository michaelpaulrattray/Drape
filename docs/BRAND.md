## The name

**Klieg** (rhymes with *league*). In 1896, two German immigrant brothers — John and Anton Kliegl — founded a stage-lighting company in New York. Their carbon-arc lamps were so bright they made indoor filming possible: early Hollywood was literally lit by klieg lights. Actors developed "klieg eyes" from their intensity, and *"under the klieg lights"* became the phrase for standing in fame's glare.

Klieg lights don't make the movie. They make the **star visible**.

The Kliegl brothers' company is long gone and "klieg light" is a generic dictionary term, so this history is ours to tell freely — no estate, no license, no lawyers.

**Brand usage:** the product and logo say **KLIEG**. "Klieg Labs" appears only where legal names belong (footer, contracts, domain). People should say *"I made this on Klieg."*

## The story (the pitch in narrative form)

Old Hollywood's studio system had one real asset, and it wasn't the cameras or the soundstages. It was the stars under contract. MGM's slogan was *"more stars than there are in heaven."* Studios didn't just film people — they **manufactured** them: the name, the look, the persona, all engineered, then deployed across films, magazines, and billboards.

The AI industry rebuilt the cameras first. Runway, Higgsfield, ElevenLabs — brilliant tools that generate footage. But footage is disposable. What compounds in value is the **face people recognize**: the character who shows up in Tuesday's reel, Thursday's ad, and next month's short film and is unmistakably the same person.

**Klieg is the star system for AI talent.** You cast a model — a face, a body, a presence that has never existed before and belongs entirely to you. You dress them, direct them, and put them to work: campaigns, UGC, reels, films. They never age out, never miss a call time, never renegotiate. Every appearance makes them more valuable, because audiences don't follow footage — they follow *someone*.

Everyone else sells the camera. **Klieg signs the stars.**

## Target audiences

### 1. Brand & marketing teams (primary, revenue)

Small-to-mid brands and in-house/agency marketing teams who need a constant stream of ad creative, UGC-style content, and product imagery — and for whom real shoots are the bottleneck: casting, booking, shipping product, usage rights, reshoots.

- **What they buy:** a consistent brand face (or roster of faces) they fully own; product-on-talent imagery without a shoot (the fashion/wardrobe DNA — real garments digitized and worn by their AI talent); ad and UGC video volume at software cost.
- **What they fear:** off-brand output, inconsistent faces between assets, legal ambiguity over likeness rights, "AI slop" aesthetics.
- **Copy angle:** *Your brand's face, on payroll forever.* Consistency, ownership, and taste — not "generate anything."

### 2. Small creators & solo builders (primary, volume/community)

Individual creators and tiny teams building AI influencers, faceless-to-faced channels, reels, and short films. The person who wants to run a virtual talent as a business, or tell stories without a cast and crew.

- **What they buy:** a persistent character with real continuity (same face, same wardrobe logic, episode after episode); a studio pipeline in a browser — cast → dress → shoot → iterate on the canvas; a path from reels to longer film work as capability grows.
- **What they fear:** character drift between generations, platform lock-in of "their" character, per-render pricing that punishes iteration.
- **Copy angle:** *Build someone worth following.* The character is the asset; Klieg is where they live and work.

**Explicit non-targets (for now):** Hollywood studios and pro VFX pipelines (Runway's turf), voice/audio-first workflows (ElevenLabs' turf), one-off image tinkerers (Midjourney et al.).

## Competitive landscape — why Klieg over…

The feature grids all converge; everyone "does everything" now. The differences that matter are identity and center of gravity.

| Competitor | Their center of gravity | Klieg's counter |
|---|---|---|
| **Runway** | Tools for AI filmmakers — owns the "AI Hollywood" tools narrative | Hollywood's asset was never the camera; it was the contract roster. Runway makes footage. Klieg makes *someone* — footage is one of the things your someone does. |
| **ElevenLabs** | Everything-suite grown from voice infrastructure | Breadth without a protagonist. In an everything-suite, a character is a feature; in Klieg, the character is the product and every tool serves them. |
| **Higgsfield** | Cinematic generation, creator-viral energy | Spectacular shots, disposable subjects. Klieg's output compounds: the same star, more famous each week. |
| **Arcads** (closest) | AI actors for performance ads | An output shop — actors are stock inventory rented per ad. Klieg talent is *owned*, persistent, and dressable in your actual product; it carries lore, wardrobe, and audience equity across every format, not just ads. |

**The moat in one sentence:** persistent, ownable, *dressable* talent — the fashion/wardrobe pipeline means real products worn convincingly by a consistent character, which no generation-first competitor treats as core.

## Voice & tone (for all copy)

Matches the product's design language: restrained, editorial, monochrome.

- **Confident, not hype.** Never "revolutionary," "unleash," "supercharge." The studio system didn't shout; it presented.
- **Talent-first grammar.** Sentences star the character, not the software: "She's booked Thursday's campaign," not "generate images of your model."
- **Old-Hollywood texture, modern restraint.** Borrow the vocabulary — cast, call time, contract, billing, debut, under the lights — sparingly, one term per surface, never costume-party.
- **Honest about artifice.** Klieg talent is proudly manufactured; the brand never pretends they're human. Manufactured stardom *is* the heritage (that's the point of the Kliegl story).
- **Less is more.** Short sentences. Monochrome imagery. One idea per section.

## Logo & visual identity (locked 2026-07-21)

**The mark:** lowercase **klieg** in a semibold neo-grotesque, tight tracking. The signature is the **bulb** — the dot of the i, rendered pure white with a soft glow: a single lamp burning over the word. It is the only lit thing, and the only warm thing, in the entire identity.

Living reference (all lockups, both polarities, flat cut, motion): `docs/brand/klieg-logo-reference.html`.

### Construction rules

- **One logo, two lighting conditions.** On dark surfaces the bulb glows. On light surfaces and in flat reproduction (print, embroidery, one-color stamp) there is no glow — the tittle is slightly oversized and raised so the signature survives as pure shape. These are the same logo, never presented as alternatives.
- **Wordmark is always lowercase.** No caps variant for "serious" contexts — ever. One name, one case.
- **Monogram / avatar:** `kı` + bulb (the k and the dotted stem). Used for favicon, social avatars, app icon.
- **Watermark on customer content:** small, corner-set, reduced opacity, glow dimmed or absent. The studio presents the work; it never joins the cast.
- **Color:** none. White on `#0A0A0A`, near-black on white. No accent color in the logo under any circumstances.
- **Motion identity:** letters illuminate left to right; the bulb lights last and brightest. This is the only logo animation, used for page loads, title cards, and video end-frames.
- **Typeface:** studies used a system stand-in (Segoe UI). Final cut requires a licensed grotesque (Söhne class) with the bulb's size, position, and glow radii tuned to its metrics.

### Guardrail

The bulb's charm is load-bearing but rationed: everything surrounding it stays severe — monochrome palette, editorial voice, hard photography, no rounded/friendly typography anywhere else. One lit bulb in a stark room is cinematic; in a soft room it's a children's app.

### Logo decision log (what was rejected)

- **Hairline-divider I** (study 01): replaced the I with a thin beam — read as "KL | EG", a law-firm partnership split.
- **Caps with lit I** (02.1/03.1): clean, but tight caps grotesque is the AI-sector uniform; not memorable.
- **Searchlight overshoot I** (02.2): the raised I read as wild/broken.
- **Gobo stencil slit** (04.3): strongest flat mark of the search, but a line through the name risks reading as crossed-out. Killed on that first-impression flaw.
- **Didone serif** (04.5): glamorous, escaped the sector uniform, but read as magazine masthead before technology company.
- **Light treatments** (04.1 spotlight pool, 04.2 sweep, 04.4 backlit flicker): kept as brand *cinematography* for heroes and title cards — they are never the logo.

## Naming decision log (why Klieg, what was rejected)

Kept for future reference so the reasoning isn't relitigated:

- **Requirement discovered:** the winners in this space (ElevenLabs, Higgsfield, Runway) don't describe their product — they sound like frontier technology companies. Names describing the *output* (glamour, fame, ads) read as agencies or consumer apps.
- **Rejected — glamour words:** Debut, Stardom, Sizzle, Gala, Jetset, Banger, Spotlight, Catchlight, Catwalk, Tinsel — all output-describing; wrong shelf.
- **Rejected — coined:** Semble, Eido — right shelf, but Klieg beat them on story.
- **Runner-up:** **Harlow** (Jean Harlow, the first fully manufactured star — born Harlean Carpenter, persona engineered by MGM). Perfect thesis fit, but she has an estate that licenses her name/likeness; telling her story in marketing invites legal risk. Klieg's history is unencumbered.
- **"Labs" suffix concern:** not an ElevenLabs clone signal — Luma Labs, Pika Labs, Sana Labs, Twelve Labs all use it; the distinctive element is the first word, and Klieg/Eleven share nothing. (Twelve Labs *does* have the clone problem — that's what a real collision looks like.)
- **Known frictions:** occasional "Kleig" misspelling (register kleiglabs.ai as a typo redirect); hard-K sound is a feature, not a bug.
