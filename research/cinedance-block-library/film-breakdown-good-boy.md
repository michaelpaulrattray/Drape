# "GOOD BOY" film breakdown — the same creator's full workflow, studied

**Filed 2026-10-12.** Source: YouTube `mJUULemmPV8` — "3 Step Workflow to Master AI Realism in 2026" (kaye.creatives, 16m37s), the creator behind the CINEDANCE block library (see README.md — the `.skill` file is this video's companion giveaway; she has viewers upload it into Claude as a custom skill). Studied by full transcript plus frame-by-frame contact sheets (1 frame/10s, all 100 frames eye-read). The film: a 9-scene emotional short — a post-apocalyptic girl must shoot her own zombified German Shepherd; a match cut takes us six years back to them playing; ~2M views, recreated by hundreds, and AI companies paid influencers to recreate it.

## Her pipeline IS our cinema studio's shape — external validation

Her three steps, in her order, with her stated reasons:

1. **The director's brief comes before any prompt.** "There's a step before the prompt that decides everything — the storyline. Beginners move straight to writing prompts for designated scenes," which loses continuity. She types the whole story to Claude "in very plain language, like the way I would tell it to my friend", with no technical aspects — and gets back one organized file: scenes, locations, both character sheets. The engine must know the general story before any scene is prompted.
2. **Assets second — characters and locations as fixed sheets.** "Most people skip this part and that is the reason why they get sloppy generations and lose character consistency." Pinterest references in, character sheets and location sheets out, generated once and reused in every scene.
3. **Scenes third**, written in plain language one after another — "what the camera does, what she does, what the doggy does, and what the scene should feel like" — then compiled by Claude into detailed video prompts, generated one at a time with the right sheets attached, and cut together externally (Premiere) under one music track.

That order — story → cast/places → takes — is the cinema studio's architecture, practiced by hand by a creator the market is paying. Her closing read of WHY companies paid: "they were buying the three hardest things in making AI videos: real emotion, realism, and character consistency." That is Klieg's casting-first bet verbatim, from the market side. And her number-one channel rule — "we never write our prompts by hand; we are lazy creative directors" — is the disappearing-technology law said by a customer: she already pays a text model to be her take compiler.

## New techniques for our record (beyond the block library)

1. **One identity, two condition sheets.** She made TWO character sheets of herself — clean/unhurt for the flashback, wounded/dirty for the present — "same face, same cap, same goggles, but with wounds and scrapes"; same for the dog (healthy vs infected, specified down to "milky white clouded eyes with almost no pupil, burst blood vessels, matted clumped fur with bald patches"). Continuity across a time jump = one identity with named condition states, never a new character. For us: a cast's signed identity needs to carry CONDITION variants (clean/wounded, young/old, before/after) that preserve the face — scene 7's whole job is proving it: "clean and unhurt, no scrapes or dirt, same cap, same goggles pushed up, same mole, and nothing drifted between the present and the past."
2. **Numbers anchor identity.** Why a prompt needs the dog's weight: "specifics are what consistency is made of — exact numbers like weight, height or age give the model strict parameters to anchor to" ("5 years old, 35 kilos, 65 cm at the withers"). Confirms the block library's numbers-over-adjectives rule applies to IDENTITY, not just staging.
3. **Location sheets carry lighting and lens.** The skipped step "90% of people get wrong": without a location sheet, every scene gets a different setting. Her location prompts specified "not only the location… but also the lighting and camera gear," so every scene shot in the supermarket shares light and glass. For our cinema: a place, once invented (§16.10 point 5: places invent freely), becomes a fixed asset whose sheet carries its lighting character.
4. **The in-prompt match cut.** Scene 5's single prompt contains BOTH halves of the match cut: first half the ball bouncing to the infected dog in the dark aisle, second half "identical framing and identical camera height, the same ball now sitting still on cracked sunlit asphalt" with the healthy dog snapping into frame — one generation producing a cut whose framing matches exactly because one prompt specified both sides. "The cut happens on the ball so your brain doesn't understand we jumped six years back." A real transition technique for the take engine.
5. **Draft-then-upscale, same take.** Her budget mechanic: generate every scene as a 480p draft first (15 credits/5s on Seedance 2.5 draft mode), and when one works, a single button re-renders THE EXACT LIKED GENERATION at 1080p (90 credits) — never a re-roll hunting for the magic again. "Play around in low settings first and only spend the big credits when you're ready." A take you liked must be finalizable in quality without changing what happens in it — a product mechanic worth weighing against our per-take pricing when cinema is built.
6. **Price on the button, live.** "The generate button displays the exact cost upfront and adjusts in real time as you edit your settings… checking the price does not cost you anything." Matches our existing law (prices on paid buttons); the real-time adjustment as settings change is the detail to keep.
7. **Fix physics with plain words, not settings.** Her failed take: the ball "just rolled — too smooth, flat, it feels dead. Technically correct, but emotionally it's not enough." The repair was a sentence: "each bounce lower and shorter than the last, kicking up dust as it goes." The iteration loop is WORDS about behaviour, not parameters — exactly the register our edit line rules for takes.
8. **Negatives against the model's habits.** "If you don't tell the model what not to do, it will add a slow push-in you never asked for. The stillness here is the point" — so "no camera movement and no zoom" goes straight in the prompt for a static shot. The take compiler should ban the model's known default behaviours whenever a shot's point is their absence.
9. **Scene prompts restate the whole character inline, every time.** Every full prompt (read off the title cards) re-carries the complete character description — bob, bangs, flight cap, goggles, choker, jacket — PLUS the attached sheets referenced in-prose as `@image_N`, plus a film-stock line ("shot on ARRI Alexa 35, 35mm, natural highlight rolloff"). Consistency is belt-and-braces: image reference AND full verbal restatement in one prompt. Our take compiler should do both invisibly.
10. **Steal the feeling, not the frames.** Her scene-writing method: she started from the saddest beat she knew (I Am Legend's dog scene) — "you are not supposed to invent the note; you're supposed to play it your way. Every filmmaker you love stole a feeling and filtered it through their own head." Design-adjacent: a reference's job can be a FEELING, which is how our film-side reference law (ingredient, not skeleton) already leans.
11. **The film needs to breathe.** Her scene 9 reasoning: "after all that joy, the film needs to breathe, so we go as far away as possible" — extreme wide, the two of them tiny, feeding him from her hand. Pacing craft the script/storyboard surface can quietly encourage (a closing wide after an emotional peak), not a rule.

## The gap her workflow shows (our opportunity)

Everything before the edit lives in two tools she stitches by hand (Claude for words, Higgsfield for pixels), with assets re-attached per scene and prompts pasted one at a time — and the final cut leaves both for Premiere. The parts she calls "the boring preparation part" (brief, sheets, locations) and the stitching are exactly what Klieg's connected casting → cinema path absorbs: cast once, places once, script derives the needs list, takes carry identity automatically, and the cut lives where the takes are. Her 17 minutes of tutorial exist because the machinery is on the customer; our product's job is for this video to not need making.
