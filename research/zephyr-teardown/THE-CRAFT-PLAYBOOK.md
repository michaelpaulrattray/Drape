# The craft playbook — the garnishes, and how a story becomes shots

**Why this document exists:** the founder caught the clause catalogue missing
the details that make outputs *rich* — the unseen light source, the causal
physics, the LOCKS recap. The miss had a mechanical cause worth naming: the
first miner ranked clauses **by frequency**, which surfaces the repeated
skeleton and buries the craft — a brilliant move used 60 times in one setup
ranks below boilerplate used 2,000 times. This pass reads for **techniques**,
each named, each with verbatim specimens and run counts. Part B answers the
second question: how a written story became prompt-language shots, scene by
scene, traced on one real scene end to end.

Everything quoted is verbatim from the corpora. This is the systemizable layer
— each technique below is a candidate function of a future Drape film engine,
so an everyday user gets these for free without being a prompt artist.

---

## Part A — the craft techniques (40)

### 1. The unseen source — light (or sound) from outside the frame

The single most cinematic trick in the corpus. Something off-frame acts on
what's in-frame; the *effect* is shown, the *cause* never is:

> *"ADDED motivated source: an **UNSEEN button/indicator off to her SIDE blinks
> RED 2–3 times — we never see the button itself, only its red light falling on
> her face/skin and the nearby metal**, pulsing red across her, then fading."*

The same move with sound: *"Haru **voice off-screen only**"* — 40+ setups play
a whole conversation against a speaker who is never in frame. And it composes
with the colour doctrine: the unseen blink gets its own budget line (*"~6%
pulsing red from the unseen button"*).

**Why it works:** off-frame causes create depth and story beyond the frame, and
they cost nothing — no extra reference, no second character render.

### 2. Countable events

Occurrences are numbered, never vague: *"blinks RED **2–3 times**"*, *"she
bounces **once** and starts talking"*, *"blink **once**, then snap to a
target"*, *"a harsh ERROR buzz on the first press; **three rapid angry
presses**"*.

**Why:** a count is checkable at the frames. "Blinks a few times" cannot fail;
"blinks 2–3 times" can — which is exactly what makes it directable.

### 3. Causal physics — consequences derived, reason stated

Not "realistic physics" as a slogan; consequences reasoned from the situation,
with the *because* left in the prompt:

> *"sweat beads form and run **from cheek toward hairline because she is upside
> down**"*
> *"an unusual CANTED/INVERTED-DUTCH angle **because the cabin is
> upside-down**"*
> *"she looks UP **(toward the floor/her boots, since she is inverted)**"*
> *"real **blood-flush from hanging inverted**"*

**Why:** the engine gets the physical *reason*, not just the effect — and every
derived detail (sweat direction, gaze target, frame tilt) stays mutually
consistent because they share one premise.

### 4. Delayed and secondary motion

The physics field's richest habit — everything soft lags the thing that moves
it: *"hair … swings gently **like a pendulum and she lags after it, slowly
settling**"*, *"sofa cushions **compress and rebound** under jumping"*, *"knees
absorb landings"*, *"strands sliding across and **sticking to her sweat-damp
forehead**"* — closed by the universal negative: *"**nothing floats or
slides**"*.

### 5. The camera has reflexes — micro-moves tied to action beats

The camera is directed as a *person reacting*, beat by beat:

> *"handheld CU throughout, tiny breathing jitter, **a micro snap-in on the
> choke, a small loosen later for her hand-flare; never cuts away**"* [108 runs]
> *"Handheld camera **jolts with her panic**"* · *"**tightens with the shift**,
> never cuts"* · *"a small **reactive reframe up as she rises**"*
> *"locked on the inverted face with micro-drift — **the operator catching the
> moment**"*

That last phrase is the mental model: not a camera path, an *operator* with
instincts.

### 6. Gaze choreography

Eyelines are directed as sequences with tempo and prohibitions:

> *"**EYELINE drifts below Jasmin → up → down to floor, slow, NO hard stare, NO
> darting**"*
> *"her **eyeline changes target with each line**"*
> *"gaze rests just BELOW Jasmin, lifts softly back UP, then settles DOWN to
> the floor; slow and deliberate, never darting"*

### 7. Named-material texture callouts

Not "detailed": named surfaces with named wear — *"webbing weave"*, *"leather
grain"*, *"**brushed metal exposed only where paint is worn through**"*,
*"fingerprinted panels, cracked aged leather"* — and light aimed at revealing
them: *"soft directional studio light **raking across the brushed metal to
reveal texture, scratches and dust**"*.

### 8. Continuity callbacks

A later setup pins itself to an established look by reference, not
re-description: *"Lighting: DIM, MOODY, low-key — **exactly the earlier
look**"*. Cheap insurance against style drift across a scene's setups.

### 9. Acting calibration — a baseline plus a sanctioned exception

The most sophisticated performance direction in the corpus:

> *"acting: **restrained Hollywood naturalism as the base; Zero's choke and
> outburst are the comedic exception**"* [173 runs]
> *"**micro-behaviour over performed emotion; the camera catches the awkwardness
> rather than the actors projecting**"*
> *"NOT theatrical, NOT a stiff CG puppet"* · *"subtle, **not stealing focus**"*

Note the structure: a default register for everyone, and an explicit licence
for the one performer allowed to break it. Background performers get their own
volume ceiling ("kept soft and out of focus, not stealing focus").

### 10. The emotion arc in one line

> *"VOICE: low female voice — alto/contralto, chesty and grounded; **calm
> baseline that cracks upward only on the outburst**"* [227 runs]

A whole performance — register, arc, and its single inflection point — in one
clause. The pattern: *state the resting state, name the one moment it breaks.*

### 11. Wetness, temperature, and their interactions

States that make surfaces alive, especially in *contrast pairs*: *"cold foggy
overcast light, **warm steam off the bowl**"*, *"condensation beads on the
steel rim"*, *"steam rises out toward her, and she blinks and **simply turns
her face away from it**"* (weather acting on performance), *"sweat-sheen,
glasses, lip ring and strap buckles **catch the light**"* (wetness serving the
lighting).

### 12. Frame exclusivity

Who and what may exist: *"**Only her own hands enter frame — NO other hands, NO
other person**"*, *"Only the pilot and the mecha are present — no other
characters"*, *"interior only, mecha never shown"*.

### 13. Sound-per-beat mapping

Every named action gets its named sound, in order — and sound carries story:

> *"a firm button-press click **then a harsh ERROR buzz/denial beep on the
> first press; three rapid angry presses**"*
> *"a sharp metallic snap/give, **a stomach-drop whoosh**, a hard CLUNK as it
> catches, rattling debris, taut-strap creak"*
> *"pebbles ticking down + **tapping the lens**"* (sound acknowledging the
> camera's physical presence)

### 14. Directed imperfection

Perfection reads as fake, so imperfection is *specified*: *"singing loud,
**off-key, a bit off-time**, roughly to the unheard beat"*, *"**imperfect
hand-felt focus**"*, *"real lens imperfections"*, *"partially undone /
imperfect fit"*, and for places: *"Scene must feel **empty but not sterile —
abandoned, not clean**"* [284 runs].

### 15. The LOCKS block — a closing contract

The mature prompts end with a recap of every non-negotiable in one block —
dual-reference sourcing, the light rule, camera bounds, the physics that must
show, the exclusions — e.g. *"LOCKS — DUAL REFERENCE (face from close-up ref
image 2, body/cabin from wide ref image 1); ZERO plastic / ZERO CG; … straps
PRESS HARD into her shoulders — show this; … camera INSIDE the cabin, never
outside; 180° honoured; no music; markers never generated."* Film one's clipped
technical tail ("85mm. 16:9. 7秒.") grew into this: same instinct, matured into
a checklist the render can be judged against.

### 16. The legibility target — `what_we_read`

The scene document names **what a viewer must be able to read off the shot**:

> *"what_we_read: Zero's forehead and brows **spiking from shock to forced-calm
> to indignation, exactly matched to each line**; her eyeline changes target
> with each line."*

This is the shot's *purpose stated as audience experience* — the single best
candidate for what a Drape film engine should ask an everyday user for, because
it is the one field a non-director can always answer: *what should someone
watching understand?*

### 17. Voice consistency without a voice reference (founder asked 2026-08-24)

In the voices-only musical scenes the attached `.wav` is a metronome the mix
excludes — so **the voices are re-invented by the engine on every take.** No
voice reference exists anywhere in the corpus — and the founder's follow-up
("did they pull the voices in as Elements?") is answered by measurement:
**all 9,460 element attachments across both later productions are images
(zero audio, zero video; the format's `video_medias` slot is empty in every
one).** An Element carries a face; nothing in this stack carries a voice. What makes them read as "the
same" is four mechanisms working together:

1. **A voice spec carried like a face spec** — `Voice:` on every sheet
   (*"soprano (high)"* / *"low, well-controlled"*) and restated in shot scopes
   (*"low female voice"*; *"calm baseline that cracks upward only on the
   outburst"*, 227 runs). A register cannot clone a voice, but it keeps each
   character in her lane and the characters distinct from each other.
2. **Fixed words, fixed tempo, melody scaffold** — verbatim lyrics in the
   prompt, 87 BPM stated, the attached track carrying the vocal hook. Same
   performance shape every take, whichever throat renders it.
3. **Directed imperfection doubles as the mask** — *"off-key, a bit off-time,
   shouty, not polished"* means there is no precise vocal identity to violate;
   group chaos hides take-to-take drift completely.
4. **The edit** — music laid under the cut, ~8s shots, one-speaker-per-shot in
   dialogue (*"only Zero speaks"*), and a zero-risk lane (*"Vocals: non-verbal
   only"*, 237 runs). No cut ever places two versions of one voice side by side.

The contrast proves the rule: in the lip-sync lane (*"`<<<audio_1>>>` plays
throughout; Mira sings lead (lip-synced)"*) the voice IS identical across 716
generations — because the file contains the performance and is audible. **Two
audio lanes: audio-as-performance (truly the same voice) and audio-as-metronome
(voice faked consistent by spec + fixed lyrics + sloppiness + the mix).** For
Drape: a `Voice:` field on the cast buys the cheap 80%; true voice identity
across takes needs a voice ASSET, which their stack lacked and they engineered
around.

### 29–40. The deep-read pass (2026-08-24, self-directed)

The founder delegated the specimen-hunting; the twelve richest unread
documents across both projects were selected per lane and read whole. Twelve
more techniques:

**29. The dedicated negative prompt.** Film one's mature prompts end with a
paste-ready negative field ("if Seedance has a negative input box, paste
here") enumerating **failure modes**: anti-composite tells (*"pasted-in look,
composited feel, Photoshop traces, cutout edges"*), anti-copy (*"same angle as
the reference, copy-paste composition"*), and viewer-comfort bounds
(*"unusable harsh shake, nausea"*).

**30. Scope a reference to its LOOK, not its composition.** *"Match
`<image_1>`'s grade, fog-blend and materials — but use a DIFFERENT camera
angle; do not copy its composition (avoid the copy-paste look)."* A new
scoping axis: grade vs geometry. One reference is designated the **GRADE
ANCHOR** (*"GRADE-UNIFY with `<image_1>`; integrated, not pasted-in"*).

**31. Weighted references.** *"Active references: `<image_1>` 100%;
`<image_4>` **90%**"* — percentage activation per reference.

**32. Directing the near-miss — and framing that reads as meaning.** The
almost-fall: name the arc (*slips → drops a short distance → caught*) and
fence the completion (*"the cable NEVER snaps, the anchor NEVER releases, NO
free-fall"*). And the deepest sentence in the corpus: *"don't push in on the
cable/anchor during the drop — **that would read as the cable snapping**."*
They know a framing choice IS a narrative statement, and fence the shot
grammar itself.

**33. Emotion fenced from both sides — and the audience's feeling as the
spec.** The cramped-cockpit two-hander names the target register (*"subtly
coquettish / playful-flirty… awkward"*) and bans BOTH neighbours: the entire
sexualization register AND the fear register (*"NOT scared, no panic"*). And
the spec is written as what the viewer must feel: *"The camera must make the
viewer feel: **'how the hell are two people fitting in there?'**"*

**34. Negatives that carry their correction.** *"spacious roomy cockpit **(it
is a CRAMPED single seat)**; entering through the glass canopy **(she enters
ONLY through the small TOP HATCH)**"* — each ban paired inline with its fix.

**35. Reference hygiene — named bleed vectors.** *"Mech/cabin images give
silhouette, colours and interior ONLY — do NOT copy any cockpit seatbelt
harness onto Haru out on the bridge — **those leak from the cockpit-seat
harness**."* They know exactly WHICH feature bleeds from WHICH reference and
fence that specific contamination.

**36. The colour doctrine is parameterized, split per set.** One scene, two
doctrines: *"cabin shots → 70% cold dark + 20% navy + 10% ember; bridge shots
→ 70% cold blue-grey + 20% warm orange-red + 10% sage/purple."* And the punk
MV inverts the whole formula (*"~70% blown-white base + ~25% the subject's own
wardrobe accent"*) — same structure, different values per piece.

**37. Same instant, three angles.** *"Hard cuts; **same moment, three
angles**… (same instant, different angles)"* — coverage that freezes story
time and varies only viewpoint. A third cutting mode beside sequence-cuts and
cross-cuts.

**38. Machine-camera motion signatures.** The device POV (#27) extended to
MOTION: *"the robot head whip-pans — fast servo motion that snaps and settles
with a small mechanical **overshoot-and-settle judder**."* Crisp axiom form:
*"**Optical character = yes; gun-sight overlay = no.**"* And the operator's
inverse: *"run-and-gun… real shoulder-mounted mass, whip-pans, breath, jolts
and **human over-correction**… never gimbal-sterile, never floaty drone."*

**39. The per-segment LENS LOCK schedule.** Six segments, six locked lenses:
*"LENS LOCK SEG1 = 107° wide … SEG3 = 29° short telephoto … SEG6 = 107° wide.
**No focal drift mid-segment.**"* Plus anti-dead-air (*"The first frame
already shows the mech center-frame, fists raised — **no empty establishing
frame**"*), one slow-mo accent with audio that reacts to it (*"slow-motion
beat **drops ambient under a low pressurized tone**"*), and *"sakuga energy"*
as a style anchor.

**40. The prompt is a collaboration document.** The most unexpected finding:
prompts carry operator-facing and team-facing writing beside the model-facing
text — an ASCII **attachment banner** (*"MUSIC / AUDIO TRACK — ATTACH IN PANEL
HERE … MAIN TRACK SLOT → `<audio_1>`"* in a box-drawing frame); **director
notes annotating deliberate default overrides** (*"(Director note: fisheye
intentionally replaces the 65mm spherical default for this piece.)"* — so
nobody 'fixes' it back); **changelog memos to teammates** (in Russian:
*"updated the layout for the new set … no dagger in this set — fully
weaponless, as you asked"*); per-character screen-time allocations
(*"(Appears rarely — shortest beat)"*); and state-override semantics on
permanent features (*"RED PLAID/TARTAN NOSE PATCH **(this state only — it
replaces her usual holographic strip)**"*). The prompt is simultaneously spec,
changelog and handoff note — the team's single source of truth per scene.

## Part B — how a story becomes shots (traced on a real scene)

The founder's question: writing a script is easy; turning it into scenes,
setups, prompt language, and knowing which props/sheets/plates you need is the
hard part. The corpus shows exactly how they do it. The lemonade-choke scene,
reconstructed from its 1,365 jobs (103 distinct setups, 2026-06-25 → 06-27):

### The pipeline, as they actually ran it

```
1. STORY BEAT (human)      "Zero chokes on her drink when Haru drops the punchline"
2. SCENE DOCUMENT          who_and_where / what_happens / tone /
                           what_we_read / what_we_do_not_show / purpose
3. ASSETS ON DEMAND        the scene names what it needs; missing things
                           are minted MID-SCENE and refined in minutes
4. REHEARSAL               blocking tests + acting tests, labelled as tests
5. COVERAGE LADDER         solo CU beats → two-shots → group framings,
                           the setup text ACCRETING precision as they learn
6. TAKES                   4–58 pulls per setup; keepers by eye
```

### The receipts, hour by hour

- **18:45** — shots running. **18:51** — *"item asset transparent rocks with
  transparent lemonade inside"* minted on `gpt_image_2`. **18:55** — next shot.
  **The prop was created mid-scene in six minutes**, then refined twice
  (*"realistic item asset whiskey cup with transparent lemonade"* → *"…white
  background"*). Props are not planned in advance; **the scene document names
  them and they are minted at first need.**
- **19:16** — *"expressive dialogue beat — CU on Zero, medium on Haru,
  brow/forehead **acting accuracy test**"* — a labelled rehearsal before real
  coverage.
- **19:36 → 21:25** — the solo coverage ladder. Watch the `purpose` string
  *accrete* as they learn: *"CU coverage, brow/forehead acting accuracy"* →
  *"single-take CU, brow/forehead acting accuracy"* → *"… **, directed
  eyelines**"*. Each refinement of the setup is a new setup; the biggest ran
  42 takes.
- **22:00 →** — two-shots: *"Zero CU with directed eyelines + brow accuracy,
  **then hard-cut Haru CU reaction**"*. Next day: Jasmin/Zero two-shots, then
  *"both framed BIG"* variants (58 takes on the winner).
- Throughout — **`what_we_do_not_show` carries continuity memory across
  scenes**: *"no real burger/food prop **(Haru mimed it earlier)**"*. The scene
  document remembers what an earlier scene established.

### What each scene-document field is for

| Field | Job | Specimen |
|---|---|---|
| `who_and_where` | cast + place, resolved to assets | *"All five pilots in `<Home>`, soft daytime, SCATTERED at different depths"* |
| `what_happens` | the action in plain words | *"Zero is mid-sip when she chokes; a drop runs down her lip"* |
| `tone` | the emotional register | *"affectionate brotherly call-out colliding with childish guilt; sharp comedic energy"* |
| `what_we_read` | **the audience-legibility target** | *"brows spiking from shock to forced-calm to indignation, exactly matched to each line"* |
| `what_we_do_not_show` | exclusions + cross-scene memory | *"no burger prop (Haru mimed it earlier); markers never drawn"* |
| `purpose` | what this generation is FOR | *"Zero dialogue beat — single-take CU"* / *"blocking test"* |

### What this means for a Drape film engine

The decomposition an everyday user cannot do is exactly the part that is
**mechanical given the scene document**: the fields above are answerable by a
non-director (*who's there, what happens, what's the mood, what should the
viewer get*), and everything downstream — the asset checklist read off
`who_and_where`, minting what's missing, the rehearsal pass, the coverage
ladder (solo CUs of each speaker → two-shots → a group frame), the craft
garnishes of Part A — is systemizable. The scene document is the interface;
the ladder and the garnishes are the engine. That is the shape of "high-quality
film-making made simple," and it is the same shape as the casting studio:
the user says what, the engine knows how.
